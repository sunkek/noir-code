How NoiR Code works

What it is

Every city keeps its secrets. This one keeps yours.

NoiR Code is a code you can scan, like a QR code, except it doesn't look like a barcode. It looks like a panel from a black-and-white crime comic: a city at night, rain, a moon behind the rooftops. The picture is the code. Your message is woven into its shadows, and the right reader can pull it back out.

Making one

Write it down. Let the city swallow it.

  1. Open NoiR Code and type your message: a note, a name, a link.
  2. Press Encode. A new night scene is drawn around your words.
  3. Download the picture and use it anywhere: print it, post it, pin it to a wall.

Short messages make smaller, simpler panels. Longer ones fill a bigger city. One panel holds up to about 170 letters of English text, enough for a sentence or a link.

Reading one

Every panel talks, if you know how to listen.

Open NoiR Code and choose Decode. Then either:

  • upload a photo or a screenshot of the panel,
  • paste a link to an image of it, or
  • point your camera at it and hold steady.

The panel can be tilted, photographed at an angle, even upside down, and it still reads. Your phone's ordinary camera app or QR scanner won't recognise it, though: NoiR Code speaks its own language, and only its own reader knows it.

How the message hides

Look closer. The shadows aren't random.

Behind the art sits a grid of tiny squares. Each square is painted one of a few shades of grey, from pale to deep black, and each shade stands for a small piece of your message. The city, the rain and the moon are drawn around those squares. They make it a picture, but only the squares carry words.

The heavy black frame around the panel is how the reader finds it in a messy photo. Once found, the reader straightens the picture out, reads the shade of every square, and turns the shades back into text.

Every message also carries extra repair information, mixed in and spread across the whole panel. If glare, a crease or a thumb hides part of the picture, the reader uses it to rebuild the missing pieces from the rest. In testing, a panel still reads with about a tenth of it covered up.

Questions

Is it a QR code?

It does the same job, but it is its own design and doesn't follow the QR standard. That's what lets it look like a comic panel instead of a barcode.

How much can I fit?

About 170 letters of English. Some characters take more room than others: Cyrillic letters take twice the space, and emoji take more still.

Is my message secret?

Hidden, not locked. Anyone who has the panel and opens NoiR Code can read it, the same way anyone can scan a QR code. Don't put passwords or private information in one.

Do you keep what I encode?

No. Panels are drawn and read on the fly, and there is no database to keep them in.

Does it cost anything?

It's free, and the code behind it is open source on GitHub.
